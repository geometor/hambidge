The Series of Root Rectangles (:math:`\sqrt{1}, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{4}, \sqrt{5}`)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. folio:: Diagonal pp. 18–21
   :page: 18

The area of a square described on the diagonal of a square is double the area of the smaller square. The rectangle obtained by taking the side of a unit square as end and its diagonal as side has ratio :math:`1 : \sqrt{2}` (:math:`1 : 1.4142\dots`). The square on the end of this rectangle is one-half the area of the square on the side. The end and side of a root-two rectangle are incommensurable, not measurable one by the other. **They are, however, commensurable in square.** (See the Greek classification of incommensurables, :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, *Elements*, Book X, Def. 2, :cite:`euclid:elements`.)

For purposes of design the most important element of a rectangle is its diagonal. The area of the square upon the diagonal of any rectangle is equal to the areas of a square upon its end and a square upon its side. This important fact is the 47th proposition of the first book of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` and has been picturesquely described by :person:`Kepler <kepler>` as "a measure of gold."

By swinging the diagonal of the :math:`\sqrt{2}` rectangle down to the baseline with a compass, we obtain the :math:`\sqrt{3}` rectangle (:math:`1 : 1.7320\dots`), whose diagonal in turn yields :math:`\sqrt{4} = 2` (composed of two unit squares), and the diagonal of the :math:`1 : 2` rectangle yields the :math:`\sqrt{5}` rectangle (:math:`1 : 2.2360\dots`).

.. _hambidge-reciprocals:

Reciprocal Rectangles and Orthogonal Diagonals
----------------------------------------------

The element of the rectangle second in importance to the diagonal is the diagonal of a :term:`reciprocal <reciprocal-rectangle>`. The reciprocal of a rectangle is obtained by dividing the side into the end (:math:`1 / \sqrt{n}`):

* The reciprocal of a :math:`\sqrt{2}` rectangle has ratio :math:`1 / \sqrt{2} = 0.7071\dots` and divides the parent :math:`\sqrt{2}` rectangle into **two** equal similar :math:`\sqrt{2}` rectangles.
* The reciprocal of a :math:`\sqrt{3}` rectangle has ratio :math:`1 / \sqrt{3} = 0.5773\dots` and divides the parent :math:`\sqrt{3}` rectangle into **three** equal similar :math:`\sqrt{3}` rectangles.
* The reciprocal of a :math:`\sqrt{4}` (:math:`1 : 2`) rectangle has ratio :math:`1 / 2 = 0.5000` and divides the parent rectangle into **four** equal :math:`1 : 2` rectangles.
* The reciprocal of a :math:`\sqrt{5}` rectangle has ratio :math:`1 / \sqrt{5} = 0.4472\dots` and divides the parent :math:`\sqrt{5}` rectangle into **five** equal similar :math:`\sqrt{5}` rectangles.

Three simple classical constructions establish the reciprocal of any dynamic rectangle: (1) applying a unit square on the end of the rectangle so its inner side cuts the main diagonal at :math:`D`, determining the reciprocal end; (2) erecting a perpendicular :math:`BC` to the main diagonal :math:`AB` at vertex :math:`B`; or (3) describing a semicircle on the end of the rectangle cutting the main diagonal at :math:`C` (Euclid III.31), from which the line through :math:`C` from the opposite end corner traces the diagonal of the reciprocal.

This strange power of modulation possessed by dynamic symmetry is shown in the symmetry of the plant. Professor :person:`Church <church>`, in describing the phyllotaxis of the sunflower, speaks of the change in cross-curve numbers from the flowers lowest on the stalk to those highest and mentions the pairs :math:`(13, 21)`, :math:`(21, 34)`, :math:`(34, 55)`, :math:`(55, 89)`; these combinations all produce the same ratio, and modulate seamlessly within the root-five and whirling-square system.
