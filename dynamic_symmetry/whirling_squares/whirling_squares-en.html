The Root-Five Rectangle and the Rectangle of the Whirling Squares¶

Jay Hambidge — The Elements of Dynamic Symmetry, Lesson I (The Diagonal, Vol. I, No. 1, Nov. 1919, pp. 15–17)

In This Section

  • The Root-Five Rectangle

The Root-Five Rectangle¶

f. Diagonal pp. 15–17 · p. 15

The basic shape derived from this summation ratio of plant growth and human proportion is a rectangle which has been given the name “root-five” (root-rectangle). It is so called because the relationship between the end and side is as one to the square root of five, $1 : 2.2360\dots$ ($1 : \sqrt{5}$). As a length unit the end cannot be divided into the side of a root-five rectangle, because the square root of five is a never-ending fraction. We naturally think of such a relationship as irrational. The Greeks, however, said that such lines were not irrational in power, because they were commensurable or measurable in square. This is really the great secret of Greek design. In understanding this measurableness of area instead of line the Greek artists had command of an infinity of beautiful shapes which modern artists are unable to use. The relationship between the end and side of a root-five rectangle is a relationship of area and not line, because as lengths one cannot be divided into the other, but the square constructed on the end of a root-five rectangle is exactly one-fifth of the area of the square constructed on the side.

Construction of the Root-Five Rectangle. Draw a unit square $BC$ and bisect one side at $A$. Draw a line from $A$ to an opposite corner $B$ (having length $\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}$) and use this line as a radius to describe a semicircle cutting the extended baseline at $D$ and $E$. Then $DE$ is twice the length of $AB$, namely $\sqrt{5}$. Complete the rectangle on $DE$: it is composed of the central unit square $CB$ flanked by two reciprocal golden rectangles of width $\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2} = 0.6180\dots$ ($\sqrt{5} = 1 + 2 \phi^{-1}$).

The Rectangle of the Whirling Squares¶

The root-five rectangle produces a great number of other shapes which are measurable in area with themselves and with the parent shape. The principal one of these is that which is called the rectangle of the whirling squares. Draw a unit square $CB$, bisect its base at $A$, draw the diagonal radius $AB$ to an upper corner, and swing $AB$ down to $E$ on the extended baseline. Complete the rectangle $DE$: its ratio is $1 : \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} = 1 : 1.6180\dots$, equal to the root-five rectangle minus one of its two flanking $0.6180$ rectangles.

The Reciprocal and the Whirling Gnomons. A process connected with the science of plan-making which was thoroughly understood by the Greeks was that of determining the reciprocal of a rectangle. Briefly, the reciprocal of a rectangle is a similar shape, custom-scaled so that the end of the larger rectangle becomes the side of the smaller reciprocal. All Greek constructions for reciprocals depend upon the fundamental theorem that the diagonal of a reciprocal always cuts the diagonal of the major shape at right angles ($90^\circ$).

In a whirling-square rectangle ($1 : \phi$), the reciprocal has area $\frac{1}{\phi} = \phi - 1 = 0.6180\dots$, and the area in excess of the reciprocal is an exact unit square (gnomon). If we draw a diagonal of the whole rectangle and a perpendicular diagonal of its reciprocal, and continue cutting off successive reciprocals inside the remainder, the cut-off squares $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots$ arrange themselves in a spiral whirling to infinity around the intersection pole or “eye” $O$—matching D'Arcy Thompson's gnomonic construction in Chapter XI (Fig. 252). Also, two whirling-square rectangles ($1.6180 + 1.6180 = 3.2360$) overlapping to the extent of a central unit square ($3.2360 - 1 = 2.2360$) reconstitute the parent $\sqrt{5}$ rectangle!
