The Series of Root Rectangles ($\sqrt{1}, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{4}, \sqrt{5}$)¶

f. Diagonal pp. 18–21 · p. 18

The area of a square described on the diagonal of a square is double the area of the smaller square. The rectangle obtained by taking the side of a unit square as end and its diagonal as side has ratio $1 : \sqrt{2}$ ($1 : 1.4142\dots$). The square on the end of this rectangle is one-half the area of the square on the side. The end and side of a root-two rectangle are incommensurable, not measurable one by the other. They are, however, commensurable in square. (See the Greek classification of incommensurables, Euclid, Elements, Book X, Def. 2, euclid:elements.)

For purposes of design the most important element of a rectangle is its diagonal. The area of the square upon the diagonal of any rectangle is equal to the areas of a square upon its end and a square upon its side. This important fact is the 47th proposition of the first book of Euclid and has been picturesquely described by Kepler as “a measure of gold.”

By swinging the diagonal of the $\sqrt{2}$ rectangle down to the baseline with a compass, we obtain the $\sqrt{3}$ rectangle ($1 : 1.7320\dots$), whose diagonal in turn yields $\sqrt{4} = 2$ (composed of two unit squares), and the diagonal of the $1 : 2$ rectangle yields the $\sqrt{5}$ rectangle ($1 : 2.2360\dots$).

Reciprocal Rectangles and Orthogonal Diagonals¶

The element of the rectangle second in importance to the diagonal is the diagonal of a reciprocal. The reciprocal of a rectangle is obtained by dividing the side into the end ($1 / \sqrt{n}$):

  • The reciprocal of a $\sqrt{2}$ rectangle has ratio $1 / \sqrt{2} = 0.7071\dots$ and divides the parent $\sqrt{2}$ rectangle into two equal similar $\sqrt{2}$ rectangles.

  • The reciprocal of a $\sqrt{3}$ rectangle has ratio $1 / \sqrt{3} = 0.5773\dots$ and divides the parent $\sqrt{3}$ rectangle into three equal similar $\sqrt{3}$ rectangles.

  • The reciprocal of a $\sqrt{4}$ ($1 : 2$) rectangle has ratio $1 / 2 = 0.5000$ and divides the parent rectangle into four equal $1 : 2$ rectangles.

  • The reciprocal of a $\sqrt{5}$ rectangle has ratio $1 / \sqrt{5} = 0.4472\dots$ and divides the parent $\sqrt{5}$ rectangle into five equal similar $\sqrt{5}$ rectangles.

Three simple classical constructions establish the reciprocal of any dynamic rectangle: (1) applying a unit square on the end of the rectangle so its inner side cuts the main diagonal at $D$, determining the reciprocal end; (2) erecting a perpendicular $BC$ to the main diagonal $AB$ at vertex $B$; or (3) describing a semicircle on the end of the rectangle cutting the main diagonal at $C$ (Euclid III.31), from which the line through $C$ from the opposite end corner traces the diagonal of the reciprocal.

This strange power of modulation possessed by dynamic symmetry is shown in the symmetry of the plant. Professor Church, in describing the phyllotaxis of the sunflower, speaks of the change in cross-curve numbers from the flowers lowest on the stalk to those highest and mentions the pairs $(13, 21)$, $(21, 34)$, $(34, 55)$, $(55, 89)$; these combinations all produce the same ratio, and modulate seamlessly within the root-five and whirling-square system.
